## The Challenge: Leading Without Reporting Lines

It was July 2025 at Canaries Solutions. I had been asked to coordinate a critical digital initiative across multiple departments.

The challenge: None of the people I needed to coordinate reported to me.

I needed to work with:
- Senior clinicians with 20+ years of experience
- Department administrators with established authority
- Finance managers with budget control
- Technical leads with their own teams

Everyone had their own reporting lines. Everyone had their own authority. I had... the project.

My initial meetings were awkward. I'd propose something, and there'd be silence. Then someone would say:
"I need to check with my director before we proceed"
"This needs approval from finance"
"Our clinical governance process requires committee review"

I felt powerless. I couldn't direct. I couldn't mandate. I couldn't command.

**I realized: I'm not leading by authority. I need to lead by influence.**

## The Metaphor: The Convener vs. The Chair

Let me explain with a metaphor that shifted my understanding.

**Leading with authority is like being the Chair of a meeting.**

You have:
- Recognized position
- Control over the agenda
- Authority to make decisions
- Power to direct action

People follow because they're supposed to.

**Leading without authority is like being a Convener.**

You have:
- No formal position
- No control over others
- No authority to decide or direct
- Only responsibility for an outcome

People follow only if they're convinced it's in their interest.

**The key difference: The Chair commands. The Convener influences.**

The Chair gets compliance through authority. The Convener gets commitment through influence.

## The Struggle: The Authority Mindset Trap

I'll be honest: I initially tried to act like I had authority.

I sent directive emails: "Please complete X by Y date."

I set deadlines: "We need to deliver this by that date."

I assigned tasks: "Your team should do this."

I was acting like a Chair... but nobody was obligated to listen.

**The result:** Silence. Slow responses. Excuses. Passive resistance.

People were polite but uncommitted. They'd say "I'll try" or "We'll see what we can do"—not "we will."

I was frustrated. I couldn't understand why my clear requests weren't being met.

Then a mentor shared something that changed everything:

"People don't follow your requests," he said. "They follow your lead. There's a difference."

"You're trying to compel through requests. You need to influence through shared interest."

**Requests get compliance. Influence gets commitment.**

## The Transformation: From Command to Influence

I completely changed my approach. I stopped trying to command. I started learning to influence.

### Principle 1: Find Shared Interest First

**Before:** I told people what I needed them to do.

**After:** I started conversations by asking about their goals.

**Question:** "What are you hoping to achieve through this initiative?"

**Answer:** Clinical team wanted to reduce administrative time to focus on patient care. Finance team wanted to see clear ROI before committing resources. Technical team wanted to avoid rework and change requests.

**Alignment:** Once I understood their goals, I could show how the project served their interests—not just mine.

### Principle 2: Build Relationships Before Initiatives

**Before:** I jumped straight to project initiatives and requests.

**After:** I invested time building relationships with key stakeholders.

**What I did:**
- Regular coffee/virtual chats (no agenda)
- Asked for their perspective on their challenges
- Shared relevant information that might help them
- Celebrated their wins and achievements

**Result:** When I needed collaboration, people knew and trusted me. They weren't helping me because I had authority—they were helping someone they knew and respected.

### Principle 3: Frame Initiatives Around Their Success, Not Yours

**Before:** "We need to implement this feature to complete my project."

**After:** "This feature would help your team [specific benefit] and align with [their goal]. How can we make this happen together?"

**Example:**
Instead of: "Implement digital logging to complete project"
I said: "Digital logging would reduce your administrative documentation time by 40%, giving you more time for patient care. This aligns with our goal of improving clinical workflows. How can we make this happen?"

**Result:** People weren't doing something for my project. They were doing something for themselves.

### Principle 4: Create Social Commitment, Not Individual

**Before:** I sent individual requests and followed up individually.

**After:** I created group forums where commitments were made publicly.

**What I did:**
- Brought together the people who needed to coordinate
- Facilitated a discussion about what needed to happen
- Asked for public commitments: "Who can do this by when?"
- Documented commitments visibly

**Result:** When people commit publicly, they follow through. Social commitment creates accountability that individual requests don't.

### Principle 5: Provide Value Before Asking for Support

**Before:** I asked for support and resources before demonstrating value.

**After:** I started by showing how I could help them.

**What I did:**
- Research their challenges before meeting
- Come with ideas or solutions
- Offer help with their priorities, not just mine
- Build reciprocity: help first, ask later

**Result:** People were more willing to support my initiatives because I'd supported theirs.

## The Real-World Application: Healthcare Digital Initiative

Let me share how this transformed our initiative.

### Before Influence-Based Approach

**My approach:**
- Sent directive emails with deadlines
- Scheduled meetings where I set the agenda
- Assigned tasks and followed up individually
- Emphasized project requirements and dates

**Results after 2 months:**
- Response rate to requests: 40% (many ignored, slow responses)
- Commitment to timelines: Low (lots of "we'll see", "we'll try")
- Relationship quality: Transactional (people did minimum required)
- Progress: Slow (blocked by lack of commitment)

### After Influence-Based Approach

**My approach:**
- Started with relationship building conversations
- Framed everything around their success
- Created forums for public commitment
- Provided value first, then asked for support

**Results after 2 months:**
- Response rate to requests: 85% (high engagement)
- Commitment to timelines: High (clear commitments, followed through)
- Relationship quality: Collaborative (people invested in success)
- Progress: Fast (unblocked by commitment)

## The Framework: Influence-Based Leadership Practices

### Practice 1: Understand Before You Ask

**Before you make requests:**
- What are their goals?
- What are their challenges?
- What motivates them?
- What do they care about?

**Action:** Have conversations that focus on learning, not requesting.

### Practice 2: Align Your Initiative With Their Success

**For every request or initiative:**
- How does this help them?
- What's in it for them?
- How does this align with their goals?

**Action:** Frame everything around shared interest.

### Practice 3: Build Reciprocity

**Before you ask for support:**
- How can you help them first?
- What problems can you solve for them?
- What value can you provide?

**Action:** Give before you take. Build reciprocity.

### Practice 4: Create Public Commitments

**Instead of:** Individual requests followed up privately

**Do:** Bring relevant parties together, facilitate discussion, ask for public commitments.

**Action:** Make commitments visible and social.

### Practice 5: Acknowledge and Celebrate Shared Success

**When things succeed:**
- Credit the people who made it happen
- Celebrate the collaborative achievement
- Document and share the success

**Action:** Reinforce that we succeeded together.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Mistake 1: Trying to Command Without Authority

**The Problem:** Sending directives as if you have authority

**The Fix:** Make requests as proposals, not commands. "Would you be willing to..." not "You need to..."

### Mistake 2: Focusing on Your Goals, Not Theirs

**The Problem:** Framing everything around your project's success

**The Fix:** Frame around how it helps them succeed in their priorities.

### Mistake 3: Neglecting Relationship Building

**The Problem:** Jumping straight to work and requests

**The Fix:** Invest time building relationships before you need collaboration.

### Mistake 4: Making Individual Requests Instead of Social Commitments

**The Problem:** Asking people individually with no visibility to others

**The Fix:** Create forums where commitments are made publicly.

### Mistake 5: Taking Credit for Shared Success

**The Problem:** "We delivered this" (when you couldn't have delivered alone)

**The Fix:** Always credit the people who made it happen.

## The Hard Truth

Influence-based leadership is harder than authority-based leadership—at first.

With authority, compliance is easy. People do what they're told.

With influence, commitment is earned. People do what they believe in.

But here's the thing: **compliance is fragile. Commitment is resilient.**

With authority, people do the minimum to satisfy the requirement.

With influence, people invest their energy and creativity because they're committed to shared success.

The best leaders I've seen—whether they have authority or not—are the ones people choose to follow.

**You can't demand commitment. You can only inspire it.**

## KEY TAKEAWAY IN ONE SENTENCE

Leading without authority isn't about finding ways to command people—it's about learning to influence them by aligning their success with yours.
