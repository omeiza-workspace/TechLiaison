# Why I Chose Application Support Over Pure Development

**Published: March 2013 | Reading Time: 5 minutes**

---

## A Personal Reflection on Career Direction

When I graduated and started my first job as a Software Developer at Imagination Tools, I thought I had it all figured out. I loved code. I loved building things. I loved the satisfaction of seeing a feature come to life after hours of programming.

But within my first year, something happened that shifted my entire perspective.

## The Eye-Opening Moment

It was a Tuesday morning when Sarah, one of our key users, came to me with a problem. The application I had spent weeks building wasn't working for her. Not because of a bug—I had tested it thoroughly. It was working exactly as I designed it.

The problem was that I had designed it for how I *thought* people should work, not for how Sarah actually worked.

I remember feeling defensive at first. "It works! The requirements were clear!" But as I sat with Sarah and watched her navigate the application—struggling through workflows that made perfect sense to me—I realized something profound:

**I was building features, not solutions.**

## The Pivot

I made a decision that some of my developer friends questioned: I started spending more time in application support than development. I wanted to understand how people actually used the systems I helped build.

This wasn't about writing fewer lines of code. It was about writing more *meaningful* code.

Over the next two years, I learned things that no computer science degree teaches:

- Users don't care about elegant algorithms; they care about getting their job done
- The most common errors aren't technical—they're usability issues
- Documentation isn't optional; it's the difference between adoption and abandonment
- Incident management teaches you what actually breaks, not what you think breaks

## What Support Experience Gave Me

Three years into my career, looking back, I can see how those early support experiences shaped everything I do today:

### 1. Empathy Before Architecture
Before I design a system, I think about who will use it. Not as abstract "users," but as real people with frustrations, deadlines, and better things to do than learning my interface.

### 2. Patterns from Problems
Every support ticket is a data point. When you see the same problem 50 times, you stop patching it and start redesigning it. This is how genuine system improvement happens—not through brainstorming sessions, but through listening to users.

### 3. Communication Skills Matter
I've learned to explain technical issues without jargon. I've learned to ask better questions to uncover real requirements. I've learned that sometimes the solution isn't technical at all—it's a training issue or a workflow change.

### 4. Humility in Engineering
Support work taught me that I don't know everything. Users will find edge cases I never imagined. Systems will fail in ways I didn't predict. And that's okay—if I'm humble enough to listen, learn, and iterate.

## The Unexpected Achievement

Here's something I never expected: my support experience has made me a better developer.

When I build systems now, I'm not just thinking about code quality. I'm thinking about:
- What happens when this fails?
- How will this be maintained six months from now?
- What documentation will someone need to fix this without calling me?
- How can I make error messages helpful instead of confusing?

These aren't secondary considerations. They're the difference between systems that work and systems that work *well*.

## Advice for New Developers

If you're starting out in tech, I have some unconventional advice: **Spend time in support.**

It might not seem glamorous. It might feel like you're stepping away from "real" development work. But I promise you this:

You'll become a better engineer. You'll build solutions instead of features. You'll understand that code is a tool, not an end in itself.

And maybe, just maybe, you'll discover—as I did—that supporting users through their problems is one of the most meaningful things we do in this industry.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Support experience builds empathy, which leads to better user-centered design
- Incident patterns reveal genuine system improvement opportunities
- Communication skills are as important as technical skills
- Humility and continuous learning come from seeing real user struggles
- Support work isn't a detour from development—it's a foundation for better development
